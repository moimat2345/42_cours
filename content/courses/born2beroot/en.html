System administration · Born2beRoot

The basics of Born2beRoot

Install a Linux server in a virtual machine, lock it down with strict rules, then be able to explain every setting in your defense.

subject: Born2beRoot · version 4 · VirtualBox (or UTM) · Debian or Rocky · submission: signature.txt

01

The project in short

Born2beRoot is your first server. You install a minimal Linux in a virtual machine, with no graphical interface, then you lock it down: an encrypted disk split with LVM, SSH on port 4242, a firewall, strict passwords, a constrained sudo, and a monitoring script that prints the state of the system on every terminal.

You hand in only one file: signature.txt, the SHA1 signature of your VM's disk. Everything else is judged in the defense: you will be asked to create a user, change the hostname, stop your script… The subject says it itself: understand what you use (p. 8).

📖

The page numbers in parentheses, like (p. 8), are the ones printed at the bottom of the subject's pages (version 4). Keep the PDF open next to this page: this course explains the subject, it does not replace it.

02

The project map

VM

The virtual machine

VirtualBox, or UTM if you cannot use VirtualBox (p. 4). Debian stable (neither testing nor unstable) or Rocky stable; Debian is recommended if you are new to system administration (p. 7).

GUI

No graphical interface

Installing X.org or any equivalent means a grade of 0. You install the minimum of services (p. 7).

LVM

Encrypted disk + LVM

At least 2 encrypted partitions using LVM; the sizes are up to you (p. 7 and 8).

SSH

Port 4242, no root

The SSH service listens on port 4242 and refuses root login. It will be tested in the defense by setting up a new account (p. 8).

FW

Active firewall

UFW (Debian) or firewalld (Rocky). Only port 4242 stays open, and the firewall is active when the VM starts (p. 8).

Users

Hostname and users

Hostname = your login + 42 (e.g. wil42), which you will modify in the defense. A user named after your login, in the groups user42 and sudo, in addition to root (p. 8).

Pwd

Password policy

Expiration, minimum delay, warning, length, complexity, repeats, username, difference from the old password (p. 8 and 9).

sudo

sudo under strict rules

3 attempts, custom message, inputs and outputs archived in /var/log/sudo/, TTY mode, restricted paths (p. 9).

cron

monitoring.sh

A bash script broadcast to all terminals with wall, at startup then every 10 minutes. You must be able to interrupt it without modifying it (p. 10).

Bonus

Partitions, WordPress, a service

A 7-volume partition layout, a WordPress website (lighttpd, MariaDB, PHP) and a useful service of your choice, NGINX and Apache2 excluded. Only evaluated if the mandatory part is PERFECT (p. 12 and 13).

Submit

signature.txt, nothing else

At the root of the Git repository. The VM is never submitted (p. 14). Snapshots are forbidden: a grade of 0 if there is one (p. 15).

The subject also announces questions about the system you chose (p. 8). Two classics to prepare:

📦

apt vs aptitude

  • ⌨️apt: the everyday command-line tool to install and update.
  • 🧭aptitude: another package manager, with an interactive text-mode interface and a more advanced dependency resolver.
  • 🧱Both rely on the same package system, dpkg. Try them and put the difference into your own words.
🛡️

AppArmor vs SELinux

  • 🔒Two kernel security modules: they limit what each program is allowed to do, even when it runs as root.
  • 🐧AppArmor (Debian): one profile per program, written around file paths.
  • 🏷️SELinux (Rocky): labels on files and processes, plus a policy that says who can do what.
  • 🚦The subject requires them to be running at startup (p. 7). On Rocky, you do not have to set up KDump (p. 7).
03

Encrypted partitions and LVM

The subject requires at least 2 encrypted partitions using LVM (p. 7) and sets no size: it is up to you to choose them so that everything works without wasting space (p. 8). Its example stacks four layers, which lsblk prints as a tree:

disk

sda

Your VM's virtual disk.

part

sda1, sda2, sda5

sda1 is mounted on /boot and is not encrypted: the boot process has to read the kernel before you have typed your passphrase. sda2 (1K) is the extended partition, a simple container; sda5 is the logical partition that gets encrypted.

crypt

sda5_crypt

The content of sda5 once unlocked with the passphrase. For lsblk, its type is crypt.

lvm

The logical volumes

LVM cuts the encrypted space into volumes (root, swap, home…), which lsblk files under the type lvm.

LVM rests on three terms: a physical volume (here sda5_crypt), a volume group that pools this space (LVMGroup in the bonus screenshot), and logical volumes that you mount like partitions. Its benefit: a volume can be resized later without repartitioning the disk. Play with the simulator: without the bonus, three volumes; with the bonus, seven (p. 7 and 12).

Demo: the simulator draws the lsblk tree of your VM according to the disk size and the bonus, then warns you when the volumes add up to more than the encrypted space. Without JavaScript, here are the subject's two examples.

What the tree teaches you:

1Mandatory part. root on /, swap on [SWAP], home on /home (p. 7).
2Bonus. Four volumes are added to the first three: var, srv, tmp and var-log, mounted on /var, /srv, /tmp and /var/log. Seven volumes in total (p. 12).
3The double dash. The var-log volume is displayed as LVMGroup-var--log: the device name doubles the dashes of the volume name. It is not a typo (screenshot p. 12).
4The sizes are arbitrary. The subject repeats it (p. 8 and 12): work out what each volume needs, and check that the total fits in the encrypted space, like the demo's indicator.
💡

For the bonus, look at what the guided partitioning assistant offers: its preset layouts (for example /home, /var and /tmp separate) do not give the seven volumes requested. Plan on manual partitioning, and check the result with lsblk, the command of the subject's example (p. 7).

04

SSH and firewall

SSH gives you remote access to the server. The subject sets two requirements: the service listens on port 4242 and you cannot log in as root over SSH (p. 8). The evaluator will test SSH by setting up a new account: you must understand how it works (p. 8).

The file to read is the server's, /etc/ssh/sshd_config (do not confuse it with ssh_config, the client's). Look in man sshd_config for the directives that deal with the port and root login. Two practical reminders: a change is only read when the service is reloaded, and to connect from your own machine, a VM on a NAT network needs a port forwarding rule in the hypervisor's settings.

On Rocky, the subject asks you to adapt SELinux's configuration to the project's needs (p. 7). For SSH, that means this: by default, SELinux only lets sshd listen on the ports it knows, and semanage is how you declare the new one to it.

The firewall is UFW on Debian, firewalld on Rocky (p. 8). Subject rule: only port 4242 stays open, and the firewall must be active when the VM starts (p. 8). Allow the port before enabling the firewall: otherwise, if you are connected over SSH, you lock yourself out.

05

sudo under control

sudo lets a user run a command with the privileges of another user, usually root. The subject asks you to install it and configure it according to five rules (p. 9). Each one is set in the sudoers file; edit it with visudo, which checks the syntax before saving (a mistake in this file can lock you out of sudo).

Subject rule (p. 9)Where to look in man sudoers
3 attempts at most in case of a wrong passwordthe password-related options, look for “passwd”
a custom message of your choice when the password is wronglook for “badpass”
every action archived, inputs and outputs, in /var/log/sudo/look for “log_input” and “log_output” for inputs and outputs, and “iolog_dir” for their folder (the default folder is not /var/log/sudo/). “logfile” only logs the commands typed, in a file whose folder must exist
TTY mode enabledlook for “tty”
paths usable by sudo restrictedlook for “path”; the subject gives an example list of paths (p. 9)

Do not just read: test each rule. Get the password wrong three times and look at your message; run a command with sudo and look at what appears in /var/log/sudo/. Your user must also belong to the sudo group (p. 8). Keep in mind that the #Sudo line of the monitoring script counts the commands run with sudo (p. 10): look at what your log lets you count.

06

Strong passwords

The policy has two parts, which are not set in the same place.

⏳

The lifetime

  • 📅The password expires every 30 days (p. 8).
  • ⏱️You must wait at least 2 days between two changes (p. 8).
  • 🔔The user is warned 7 days before expiration (p. 9).
  • 📚Pitfall: /etc/login.defs sets the values for accounts created afterwards. For accounts that already exist, aging is read and set with chage (man chage).
🔑

The quality

  • 🧩It is checked on every password change, thanks to a PAM module: pam_pwquality (package libpam-pwquality on Debian).
  • 📄Its settings go in /etc/security/pwquality.conf (or in the matching PAM line): one option per rule, to be found in man pwquality.conf.
  • 👤The old-password rule does not apply to root (p. 9).
check
sudo chage -l your_login  # the delays of an existing account: read them and compare with the subject

The seven quality rules written in the subject (p. 9) fit in the checker below. Try the examples, then invent your own: find a password that fails only one rule.

Demo: you type a password (and, if you want, a username and an old password); each rule of the subject turns ✓ or ✗. Here is what the demo's five examples give.

What the demo assumes. It only follows the rules written in the subject. The username is searched for regardless of case; “characters not in the old password” is read as “characters of the new one that do not appear in the old one”. The real module does its own checks on top (a dictionary, for example).

⚠️

All accounts, root included. Once the configuration files are in place, you must change the passwords of all the accounts on the VM, root included (p. 9). And root's password must follow the policy too (p. 9).

wall sends a message to all logged-in users. By default, it adds the “Broadcast message from …” line of the example above: that is the banner the subject calls optional. Read man wall to find out how to remove it.

cron runs a command at recurring times. A crontab line has five time fields; how you read them makes the difference between “every 10 minutes” and “once an hour”:

crontab: reading the five fields
# minute  hour   day   month day-of-week
*/10      *      *     *     *        # at minutes 0, 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 of every hour
10        *      *     *     *        # only once an hour, at minute 10

For the startup, man 5 crontab describes a special trigger: look for “reboot”. Finally, the defense will ask you to interrupt the script without modifying it (p. 10): cron is what launches it, so that is the one you must know how to stop or disable.

10Signature. Shut down the VM, compute the SHA1 of its disk, paste it into signature.txt at the root of the repository, then never turn the VM on again (p. 14). Make your copy before any defense.
11Prepare the defense. Create a user and assign it to a group, change the hostname, stop the monitoring, explain apt versus aptitude and AppArmor or SELinux (p. 8 and 10).

09

Classic pitfalls

⚠️

A defense modifies your VM. During the evaluation, you change the hostname and create a user (p. 8), and merely booting already writes to the disk. The signature can therefore change after the first evaluation; if it no longer matches the one in signature.txt, the grade is 0 (p. 14). The subject advises you to duplicate the VM or use save state (p. 14).

⚠️

Cloning without checking. Make the copy before starting the VM for a defense, keep a disk that you never turn on again, and compare the hash of the copy with the original's. A clone made by VirtualBox is in principle given a new disk identifier (UUID), hence a different hash (to check on your setup); a plain copy of the disk file, made with the VM off, should keep the same hash (also to check). A snapshot is not a copy: snapshots are forbidden and a single one detected means 0 (p. 15).

⚠️

Touching the VM after the signature. An update “just to check”, a configuration fixed at the last minute: the disk changes, and the signature is worth nothing. If you have to change something, recompute the signature and update signature.txt before the defense.

⚠️

sudo without logging. The subject asks you to archive every action, inputs and outputs, in /var/log/sudo/ (p. 9). Keeping only a record of the commands typed is not enough, and the default folder for input and output logs is not the one the subject requires: run a command with sudo and check that files really appear in /var/log/sudo/.

⚠️

SSH port not reloaded. You edited sshd_config but the service is still running with the old configuration: it still listens on its old port. Reload the service, then prove it with ss -tunlp (p. 11). On Rocky, think about SELinux (p. 7).

⚠️

Non-compliant root passwords. The subject spells it out: root's password follows the policy (p. 9), and all existing passwords are changed once the configuration is in place (p. 9). Trap: by default, pam_pwquality merely warns when root changes a password, without blocking it (man pwquality.conf or man pam_pwquality, depending on the version). Check every password yourself, yours and root's.

⚠️

cron that does not run at an exact interval. 10 * * * * is not “every 10 minutes”: it is once an hour. And */10 follows the clock (minutes 0, 10, 20…), not the startup time, whereas the subject's example shows 15:45:00 for a last boot at 14:45 (p. 10). Check that the cron service is active, watch the time the messages arrive, and be ready to justify your choice. Also test with cron, not only by hand.

⚠️

What the subject forbids or penalizes. A graphical server (X.org or equivalent): 0 (p. 7). A signature different from the disk's: 0 (p. 14). The VM in the repository: forbidden (p. 14). A bonus added to an imperfect mandatory part: it is not evaluated at all (p. 13). On the system side, the subject requires the latest stable version of Debian or Rocky, neither testing nor unstable (p. 7).

10

How I did it

I installed Debian in VirtualBox, with no graphical interface. I kept my notes as tables, one per topic (passwords, sudo, SSH, firewall), with the required value next to each rule. I added the list of commands that check each requirement: lsblk for the partitions, chage for the password delays, ufw status for the firewall, crontab for the scheduled task, hostnamectl for the machine name. To stop the monitoring without touching the script, I learned that it is enough to stop cron. I did not do the bonus. My repository contains only signature.txt and these notes: the VM is not in it, as the subject requires.
